# Autween — Initial Evidence Review

**Review date:** 12 November 2026  
**Scope:** Online social participation, safety, collaborative play, and adaptive technology for autistic young people.  
**Limitations:** This is an initial desk review based on selected studies and reviews. It is not a systematic review, does not establish causal efficacy, and does not replace co-design with autistic people.

## 1. Online social participation: opportunity and risk coexist

A study of autistic young people's online safety experiences reports both the value of online contact and risks including cyberbullying, online sexual harassment, and unwanted solicitation. Participants expressed interest in online connection while also wanting better controls for unwanted contact. This supports designing for connection and safety together, rather than treating social access itself as the risk.

A qualitative study of autistic young adults in Hong Kong similarly describes online social media as enabling connection while exposing participants to misunderstanding, conflict, cyberbullying, scams, unwanted sexual experiences, and privacy risks.

Research on autistic and non-autistic teenagers' privacy and safety attitudes on social networking sites highlights that platform design and social context can contribute to vulnerability. Privacy controls should therefore be understandable and usable, not merely available in settings.

**Product implication:** Start with small, approved circles, clear interaction boundaries, and accessible block/leave/report controls. Do not build an open public feed or stranger-messaging system into the first release.

## 2. Digital communication can offer greater control

A systematic review of ICT use by autistic people found that online communication may offer greater control over how and when people communicate and engage, and may be experienced as calmer by some participants. The evidence base is heterogeneous and preliminary; this should not be generalized to every autistic person.

**Product implication:** Support asynchronous interaction, multiple communication modalities, and control over response timing. Do not make live voice or rapid chat the default.

## 3. Collaborative play is promising, but not a universal prescription

A 2024 systematic review of collaborative play for autistic children reports that tablet-based activities can facilitate social interaction and that peer-mediated approaches may be useful. A 2024 review of serious games reports positive findings across some studies, but the number and quality of studies limit broad conclusions. A 2025 scoping review of game-based approaches notes inconsistent definitions and the need for autistic voices earlier in design.

**Product implication:** Shared creative activities are a reasonable design direction to prototype, not a proven intervention. The product should offer multiple types of participation and allow children to choose whether to play alone, alongside others, or collaboratively.

## 4. Adaptive AI requires restraint

A 2026 scoping review of inclusive and adaptive AI for neurodivergent users identifies potential while also noting fragmented evidence, limited long-term evaluation, and insufficient participation of neurodivergent people in design. This argues against building an opaque AI model that profiles children.

**Product implication:** Use explicit choices and simple preference estimates first. If statistical personalization is introduced, keep it narrow, explainable, reversible, and evaluated against user-reported comfort and preference—not normative social outcomes.

## 5. Design conclusions

The evidence supports investigating the following principles:
1. **Connection without exposure:** provide meaningful social participation without public discovery or unsolicited contact.
2. **Choice of modality:** let users choose how to express themselves.
3. **Control over timing:** support asynchronous participation and predictable interaction.
4. **Shared activity over forced conversation:** provide a reason to collaborate that does not require constant verbal exchange.
5. **Safety controls as core interaction:** block, leave, pause, and seek help must be easy to find.
6. **Co-design, not assumption:** autistic people must be involved in choosing activities, language, controls, and success measures.
7. **No normalization objective:** do not reward masking, eye contact, speech, or suppression of harmless autistic behaviours.

## 7. Research gaps

This initial review does not yet answer:
- which specific game mechanics autistic children prefer;
- what children consider a safe and welcoming peer space;
- how mixed-neurotype groups should be moderated;
- which communication tools are most useful across different support needs;
- how children want adults to participate in safety decisions;
- whether adaptive recommendations improve comfort compared with explicit settings alone.

These are product research questions, not gaps to fill by guessing.
